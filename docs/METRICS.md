# Pulse Metrics: Specification (algorithm version 6, shared formulas version 2)

This is the **single source of truth** for how Pulse computes its numbers. There are two implementations:

| Implementation | Path | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Python | `re-toolkit/metrics/` | Reference. Also runs on real band captures |
| Swift | `ios/PulseCore/Sources/PulseCore/Metrics/` | Runs in the app |

Both must give the **same results** on the shared test vectors in `shared/test-vectors/metrics_v2.json`
(generated by `re-toolkit/tools/make_metric_vectors.py`, checked by both test suites, tolerance 1e-9).

When a formula changes: bump the algorithm version, regenerate the vectors, and the app recomputes old days.

> **Honesty labels.** Each section says what comes from published research and what is **our own choice**.
> WHOOP's Recovery/Strain/Sleep formulas are proprietary. Ours are not copies; they're transparent alternatives.
> Pulse is for general wellness, not medical use.

## 0. Shared helpers (exact definitions matter for parity)

| Helper | Definition |
|---|---|
| `mean(x)` | left-to-right sum ÷ n (no compensated summation) |
| `median(x)` | sort; odd n → middle; even n → average of the two middle values |
| `sampleSD(x)` | √(Σ(x−mean)² ÷ (n−1)), needs n ≥ 2 |
| `lowerPercentile(x, p)` | `sorted(x)[floor(p × (n−1))]` |
| `Φ(x)` | standard normal CDF = 0.5 × (1 + erf(x / √2)) |

## 1. RR interval cleaning

**Why:** one missed or extra beat (common with optical sensors) creates a huge fake difference and wrecks HRV.

1. Reject every beat of **exactly 500 ms**. The band writes that value when it has no beat to report (about 5% of
   the beats on a real night). A true 500 ms beat (120 bpm) is lost too, which doesn't matter during sleep.
2. Reject RR outside **300–2000 ms** (200–30 bpm).
3. For each remaining beat, take the **median of the valid beats within ±5 positions** (11-beat window, clipped at
   the edges, including the beat itself). Reject the beat if it differs from that median by **more than 20%**.
4. Walking forward, reject a beat that differs by **more than 20%** from the clean beat right before it.

Each minute is cleaned on its own. Output keeps positions (rejected = null), so we know which beats are still adjacent.

*Source:* the 20% rule follows the classic Malik criterion used in the HRV standards (Task Force of the ESC/NASPE,
*Circulation* 1996; 93:1043–1065). Using a local median instead of the previous beat is a simplification of
Lipponen & Tarvainen, *J Med Eng Technol* 2019; 43(3):173–181.

## 2. HRV: RMSSD

`RMSSD = √(mean of (RRᵢ − RRᵢ₋₁)²)` over pairs where **both** beats survived cleaning and belong to the **same
minute** (a pair is never formed across two minutes). Needs ≥ 20 pairs.
*Source:* Task Force 1996 (RMSSD is the standard short-term vagal HRV measure).

**Overnight HRV:** walk through the sleep session minute by minute.
- Wherever **5 consecutive minutes** are all asleep (and worn), take their RR intervals as one segment, clean them,
  compute RMSSD, and jump ahead 5 minutes. Otherwise step 1 minute.
- Overnight HRV = **median** of the segment RMSSDs. Needs ≥ 3 segments.

*Source:* 5-minute windows are the Task Force standard short-term recording length. The median makes it robust to a
few noisy segments. *Our choice:* WHOOP reportedly weights the last slow-wave sleep. We can't detect sleep stages
reliably, so we use the whole night.

## 3. Resting heart rate

The **lowest mean HR over any 10 consecutive asleep minutes** in the main sleep (awake/off-wrist minutes break
the window).

*Source:* nocturnal minimum HR is a standard resting-HR proxy (Nanchen, *Heart* 2018; 104:1048–1049). The 10-minute
window is *our choice*, so that one quiet minute or an artifact can't fake a low value.

## 4. Sleep detection (a probability per minute)

Code: `SleepModel.swift` (Swift only). The model works out the chance of being asleep for every minute, from several
signals together, and learns from the owner's own nights and edits.

### Signals

| Signal | Per minute | Starting values? | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Movement** | `ln(1 + A)`, see below | yes | Actigraphy is the classic sleep/wake signal (Cole et al. 1992) |
| **Heart rate drop** | `100 × (awake HR − hr) / awake HR`, against the owner's own awake HR over the past 24 h | yes | Combining motion and heart rate beats motion alone (Walch et al. 2019) |
| **Heart rate steadiness** | SD of minute HR over the 15 minutes around this one | yes | *Our choice*: awake heart rate swings with what you are doing |
| **Clean beats** | Clean beats per minute over 5 minutes (§1) | no, learned | *Found in the owner's data*: see the table below |
| **HRV** | `ln(RMSSD)` over 5 minutes (§2) | no, learned | Parasympathetic activity rises in NREM sleep (Trinder et al. 2001; Stein & Pu 2012) |
| **Time of day** | Typical sleep hours, moved toward the owner's own | yes | Sleep timing follows a circadian process (Borbély 2022), and a clock signal helps wearables (Walch et al. 2019) |

**Movement** removes the band's resting offset. On a real night the per-second activity sat at a steady 0 to 10 for
minutes at a time, changing only when the wrist settled into a new position, so the raw value says little about
moving. Sitting awake at a computer mostly reads 3 to 11, the same range. With `s` = the median of the minute's 60 values:
`A = mean(max(0, value − s))`, or the plain mean when `s > 12` (too high to be a wrist lying still).

**Newer minutes use the wrist angle.** Each second Pulse stores the angle between that second's gravity reading and the one
before it (at most 2 seconds earlier), in tenths of a degree. With at least 30 such seconds in a minute,
`A = mean angle in degrees × 500 × π/180`, which is the scale the older formula uses. Sustained changes in arm angle are the
published way to find rest from a wrist accelerometer (van Hees et al. 2015, 2018). *Our choice:* the angle in all three
dimensions rather than the z-axis only. Minutes saved earlier keep the older formula, because the raw readings were not stored.

**Left out, on purpose:**

- **Breathing.** Breathing was faster and shallower in every sleep stage than awake in Douglas et al. 1982, so a slower rate
  is not a sign of sleep. The breathing rate Pulse shows for a night is described in §11.
- **Skin temperature.** Warming of the hands and feet does predict falling asleep quickly (Kräuchi et al. 1999), but Pulse
  does not decode or store temperature, and the band's raw value has no confirmed unit.

**How well each signal separated sleep from wake on the one real night** (AUC: 0.5 = useless, 1 = perfect):

| Signal | vs all awake minutes | vs the evening at the computer |
|---|---|---|
| Clean beats | 0.98 | 0.96 |
| Heart rate | 0.95 | 0.95 |
| Movement | 0.92 | 0.91 |
| HRV (RMSSD) | 0.45 | 0.43 |

HRV did not rise during that night's sleep. That may be the owner's physiology, or the beat-to-beat decoding, which is still
unconfirmed. Because HRV has no starting values, it only counts as far as it really separates the owner's own minutes.

### The calculation

1. **Evidence.** Each signal has one bell curve for asleep and one for awake. Its evidence for a minute is
   `0.6 × (ln density asleep − ln density awake)`, limited to ±3. Evidence is added up across signals (naive Bayes; Hand & Yu
   2001). The 0.6 allows for signals that overlap. Unless the heart rate also points to sleep, being still and having a steady heart
   rate can each add at most +0.4 toward sleep, because wearables are good at finding sleep but poor at spotting quiet
   wakefulness (Schyvens et al. 2025). A minute with no band data adds no evidence either way.
2. **Time of day.** `0.25 × logit(chance of being asleep at this minute of the day)`, limited to ±0.8. The chance is
   `(3 × typical + nights asleep at this minute) / (3 + nights)`, averaged over ±15 minutes.
3. **Smoothing.** A two-state hidden Markov model, run forward and backward over all minutes (Rabiner 1989), gives
   P(asleep) per minute. Each minute, the chance of falling asleep is 1/720 and of waking up is 1/240.
4. **Learning.** The model runs three times per week of data. After each run, minutes with P ≥ 0.8 are labelled asleep and P ≤ 0.2 awake,
   and the curves, the awake heart rate and the sleep hours are updated from those labels. Starting values count as
   300 minutes of data. A learned-only signal needs 120 labelled minutes of each kind. **Nothing is learned until there are 3
   nights of data.** Until then the model uses the starting values and the typical sleep hours, and the learned-only signals are
   off. One direct trial of personalising wrist-based sleep staging improved results with more personal nights (van der Aar
   et al. 2025). The 3 is *our choice*.
5. **The owner's edits teach it.** "I wasn't asleep" labels those minutes awake. An edited sleep labels its minutes asleep,
   and the hour on the other side of a time the owner moved as awake. Each edited minute counts as 5.

**Weeks.** The calculation runs one week at a time (17:00 to 17:00), and each week starts from what the week before
learned. A week's result depends only on its own minutes, the edits that touch it and that starting point, so a week whose
inputs haven't changed is reused from a cache and gives exactly what a full calculation would. New band data recalculates the
latest week only. An edit recalculates its own week and, if that changes what was learned, the weeks after it.

### From probability to a sleep

6. A minute is **asleep** when P ≥ 0.5.
7. **Sessions:** runs of ≥ 15 asleep minutes are merged when they are ≤ 30 minutes apart, or ≤ 120 minutes apart with at
   least 25% asleep between them. A quiet spell under 30 minutes that is followed by 10+ minutes awake doesn't start the sleep.
   Up to 30 minutes after the end, a further 5 asleep minutes extend the sleep. A sleep needs at least 45 minutes asleep.
   Outside the hours the person usually sleeps (step 12's usual hours: a typical chance of being asleep of at least 50%, moved
   toward their own nights once 3 are known), 20 minutes asleep are enough, so a daytime nap is found on its own. Inside those
   hours the 45 minutes stay, so a restless night doesn't break into short "naps".
   A sleep is a **nap** when its span is under 180 minutes, or under 6 hours when most of its span is outside the usual hours
   and a main sleep ended at most 18 hours before it started. Everything else is a main sleep, so the first sleep after a night
   up still counts as the night.
   *Our choice:* 20 minutes, the owner's rule. The 6-hour daytime rule: the owner's afternoon nap of 2026-11-19 ran from about
   15:00 to 18:20, longer than 3 hours, and must not become a second night. WHOOP calls every sleep other than the main one a nap.
8. **Main sleep of a day** = the longest non-nap session that **ends** on that calendar day.
9. **Finished:** a sleep is shown only after **15 of the 45 minutes** after it are clearly awake (`A > 2.5`, or HR 10+ bpm
   above the sleep's), or the band was off the wrist for an hour, or 6 hours have passed without newer data.
10. **Minutes inside a sleep:** unknown = no band data. Awake = `A ≥ 6`, or HR 10+ bpm above the sleep's median. An awake stretch
    shorter than 3 minutes between asleep minutes counts as asleep, so only longer ones are wake-ups. A sleep with no band data
    at all, which only the user can add (for example a nap logged by hand), counts as asleep for its whole length. The other minutes are
    asleep, and §12 splits them into deep, REM and light. Asleep time, awake time and wake-ups all come from
    this one rule, so deep + REM + light = asleep and asleep + awake + unknown = the length of the sleep. Efficiency is asleep ÷
    (asleep + awake).
    *Our choice:* 3 minutes. On the owner's night of 2026-11-17/18, 11 of the 12 wake-ups were 1 to 2 minutes long, mostly a heart-rate
    jump with no movement, and the band's signal was clean in all of them (no missing heart rate, filler beats as usual).
11. **Missing data.** A sleep starts and ends on measured minutes and needs 45 of them asleep. A gap of up to 3 hours with
    sleep on both sides stays inside the sleep as unknown. A longer gap splits it.
12. **Asking the owner.** A sleep shows "Is this right?" when at least 15% of it has no data, when at least 15% of its measured
    minutes have a P(asleep) between 0.2 and 0.8, or when most of a sleep that isn't a nap falls outside the hours the owner usually
   sleeps (a nap is outside them by definition). "Yes" labels
    the sleep's minutes asleep, "Fix times" labels them by the new times, and "Not asleep" labels them awake. These labels are
    the edits of step 5. *Our choice:* all three limits.

The user's edits ("I wasn't asleep", new start / end) are applied last and survive every recalculation. A time the user
left unchanged follows the detection, so better detection can still correct it.

`SleepDetector.coleKripke` and `classify` stay in the code as the formulas shared with the Python reference, but the app
no longer uses them to find sleep.

*Our choice:* every number in this section. The research above supports which signals to use and how to combine them. It
does not supply these values. They are set on **one** real night (2026-11-17/18, including the awake evening before it) and
simulated nights, and **must be checked against more real nights**. Holding very still with a low heart rate for a long time
can still look like sleep, which is why a sleep can be edited or removed. Sleep stages are estimates (§12).

## 5. Strain (0–21)

Banister TRIMP per minute, from mean minute HR:

`HRr = (HR − restingHR) / (maxHR − restingHR)`, clamped to ≤ 1. Minutes with **HRr < 0.2 are ignored**.
`TRIMP = Σ HRr × a × e^(b·HRr)` (Δt = 1 min), with **a = 0.64, b = 1.92** (male / unspecified) or
**a = 0.86, b = 1.67** (female).

`Strain = 21 × (1 − e^(−TRIMP / 130))`

*Sources:* TRIMP from Banister, in *Physiological Testing of Elite Athletes* (1991), and Morton, Fitz-Clarke &
Banister, *J Appl Physiol* 1990; 69(3):1171–1177. Max HR default `208 − 0.7 × age` (Tanaka, Monahan & Seals,
*JACC* 2001; 37(1):153–156).
*Our choices:* the 0.2 floor (so sitting around doesn't add up to "strain"), and the saturating 0–21 mapping with
k = 130. A hard 60-minute run (HRr 0.8) gives about 14.

Day strain uses all minutes of the calendar day. restingHR = that morning's resting HR, else the baseline mean, else 60.

## 6. Sleep need (minutes)

`need = goal (default 480) + 6 × max(0, yesterday's strain − 10) + 0.5 × max(0, yesterday's need − yesterday's sleep)`,
capped at 600.

**Naps** lower the next night's need: the minutes asleep in naps between one main sleep and the next are taken off,
`need − naps`, down to 0. That lower need is what the app shows and what tomorrow's debt is measured against. Recovery (§7)
uses the need before naps, and a nap's heart rate and HRV are never used for recovery or its baseline.

*Source of the idea:* sleep debt accumulates (Van Dongen et al., *Sleep* 2003; 26(2):117–126).
*Our choice:* the coefficients. They're simple and explainable, not fitted.

**Sleep planner.** For a wake-up time (the one chosen, else the usual one: the circular mean of the last 7 nights' wake-ups, from 3
nights) and a goal, `sleep = goal × tonight's need` (after naps) and `time in bed = sleep ÷ efficiency`, where efficiency is the mean of
the last 7 nights, kept between 75% and 100% (1 before the first night). Bedtime = wake-up − time in bed. Goals: Peak 100%, Perform
85%, Get by 70%, the three goals of WHOOP's Sleep Planner. NOOP only has a wind-down nudge (a fixed sleep need before a set wake time),
so this is Pulse's own; with Peak and the usual wake-up it gives the same bedtime as before. The bedtime reminder fires 30 minutes
before it.

## 7. Recovery (0–100%)

Needs tonight's HRV and resting HR, and **≥ 4 previous nights** in the baseline (the previous 14 days with data).
Otherwise there's no score; the app says it's still learning your normal.

```
zHRV   = (ln(HRV) − mean(ln baseline HRV)) / max(sampleSD(ln baseline HRV), 0.05)
zRHR   = (RHR − mean(baseline RHR)) / max(sampleSD(baseline RHR), 1.0)
ratio  = min(sleep / need, 1.1)
zSleep = clamp((ratio − 0.9) / 0.1, −3, 2)
c      = 0.6·zHRV − 0.25·zRHR + 0.15·zSleep
Recovery = clamp(100 × Φ(c), 1, 99)
```
Zones: green ≥ 67, yellow 34–66, red < 34.

*Sources:* comparing ln-RMSSD with a rolling personal baseline (Plews et al., *Sports Med* 2013; 43:773–781);
resting HR and HRV as complementary markers (Buchheit, *Front Physiol* 2014; 5:73); sleep and recovery
(Fullagar et al., *Sports Med* 2015; 45:161–186).
*Our choices:* the weights (0.6 / 0.25 / 0.15), the SD floors, and the Φ mapping. At your baseline it scores 50%,
and one SD better than normal scores about 84%.

### Known limitations
- **The sleep model is set on one real night.** It matches the owner's own account of that night. It has not been

## 9. Estimated daily calories

NOOP's whole-day method (`WorkoutDetector.swift`, `Calories.estimateDayEnergy`), second by second. Needs weight, height, birth year, sex
and a resting heart rate. Without them, or without band data for the day, nothing is shown.

- **Resting (revised Harris-Benedict), per day:** male `88.362 + 13.397 × kg + 4.799 × cm − 5.677 × age`,
  female `447.593 + 9.247 × kg + 3.098 × cm − 4.330 × age`. Only time the band measured counts. A gap adds at most 60 seconds, so
  hours off the wrist add nothing.
- **Fitness (Uth):** `VO2max = 15.3 × max HR / resting HR`.
- **Active (Keytel, fitness-adjusted):** for a second at or above **resting HR + 50% of heart-rate reserve**, in kJ/min:
  male `−95.7735 + 0.634 × HR + 0.404 × VO2max + 0.394 × kg + 0.271 × age`,
  female `−59.3954 + 0.450 × HR + 0.380 × VO2max + 0.103 × kg + 0.274 × age`. Divide by 4.184 for kcal. HR is capped at max HR. Only
  what it adds above the resting rate counts, and it never subtracts. A reading carries active energy for the band's usual sample
  spacing only, never across a gap.

The card says how much of the day was measured when the band covered less than 90% of it.

*Sources:* Roza & Shizgal, *Am J Clin Nutr* 1984; 40:168–182. Uth et al., *Eur J Appl Physiol* 2004; 91:111–115. Keytel et al.,
*J Sports Sci* 2005; 23:289–297. The 50% gate is NOOP's: Keytel was fitted on exercise, and a lower gate over-counts ordinary daytime
heart rates by 1000+ kcal.

**How accurate this is:** it is an estimate from heart rate. WHOOP's own formula is private, so Pulse can't match it exactly. In a
study of seven wrist devices against lab measurement, none had an energy error below 20% (Shcherbina et al., *J Pers Med* 2017; 7(2):3).
Pulse's number has not been compared with a calorimeter or with the owner's Apple Watch.

## 10. Workouts and heart-rate zones

Sources and licenses for the ported parts are in `docs/THIRD_PARTY.md`.

- **Zones:** `resting HR + share × (max HR − resting HR)` with shares 50/60/70/80/90% (Karvonen, OpenStrap `hr_zones.dart`), counted
  second by second so short peaks aren't averaged away. Resting HR is the median of recent nights. With none, zones are a share of max HR.
- **Workout calories:** active calories only: seconds at or above resting HR + 40% of heart-rate reserve, minus the resting rate
  (OpenStrap `calories.dart`). This is separate from the whole-day method in §9, and is waiting for a comparison with the owner's Watch.
- **Cardio:** HR at or above `max(resting + 40, resting + 45% of reserve)` for 12+ minutes, with dips up to 90 s bridged (OpenStrap
  `auto_detect.dart`).
- **Strength:** 20+ minutes of raised HR (average 20% or peak 30% of reserve, pauses up to 5 minutes), average movement of at least 8,
  at least 3 climbs and falls of 15 bpm, and NOOP's classifier agreeing. *Our choice:* everything except the classifier.
- **Walk suggestion:** 10+ minutes of movement of at least 30 with HR 10% of reserve above resting. Pulse asks "Was this a walk?"
  and doesn't count it until you answer. At one reading a second the rhythm of steps can't be measured. *Our choice.*

None of this has been compared with a chest strap, and the strength and walk rules have not yet been run on a real workout.

## 11. Breathing rate

OpenStrap's method (`resp_rate.dart`): breathing speeds the heart up and slows it down, so the strongest rhythm in the beat intervals
between 9 and 30 cycles a minute is the breathing rate. Each 5-minute window (overlapping by half) gets a Lomb-Scargle spectrum, and
the night's rate is the window closest to the median. At least half the windows must agree within 2 breaths a minute. Otherwise
Pulse shows `--`.

## 12. Sleep stages

NOOP's default stager (`SleepStagerV2.swift`), with its constants unchanged. Each 30 seconds of a night gets a score for each stage, and
the most likely path through the night is chosen (Viterbi, with NOOP's transition table). All `z` values are standardized within the night.

- **Signals:** `H` mean heart rate, `V` heart-rate spread over 5 minutes, `M` share of seconds moving, `R` regularity of the breathing
  rhythm in the beat intervals (0.15 to 0.40 Hz, off in Pulse, see below), `c` how far through the night, from 0 to 1.
- `deep = −1.1 zV − 0.5 zM − gate + 0.6 zR + 1.2 × max(0, 1 − c / 0.55) + ln 0.15`, with `gate = 5 × max(0, flatness rank − 0.25)` and
  flatness = heart-rate spread over 11 minutes.
- `REM = 0.6 zV − 0.6 zM + 0.4 zH − 0.6 zR + c − guard + ln 0.22`, with `guard = 3 × (1 − minutes since sleep onset / 60)`, kept between 0 and 3.
- `light = ln 0.50`.
- `awake = zM + 0.8 zV + 0.4 zH + ln 0.10`. On a still wrist the heart-rate part can only lower this. Add 2 when the peak jerk passes 55 × the
  night's median jerk. A second is moving above 38 × that median.

*Changed for this band:*
- NOOP reads the gravity vector. Pulse stores how far the wrist turned each second, so jerk = `2 × sin(angle / 2)`. Older minutes use
  activity ÷ 500. The median jerk can't go below 0.002, the smallest step those values can show.
- The band doesn't time each beat, so beat times are rebuilt from the start of each minute, and its filler value is left out.
- Step 10 of §4 still decides asleep or awake, so sleep totals don't change. NOOP's stage only splits the asleep minutes. A minute takes the
  stage of its first 30 seconds, and an asleep minute NOOP calls awake counts as light. A stage lasting under 3 minutes joins the stage
  before it.
- **The breathing term `R` is off.** NOOP's sleep-lab test used data without beat intervals, so the term never ran there and its weight
  is untested. On the owner's night of 2026-11-17/18 it moved deep from 14% to 22% of sleep and REM from 25% to 37%, past the usual adult
  ranges. Without it the stager runs as NOOP tested it.

*Accuracy:* NOOP scored this stager against sleep-lab results for 31 people (PhysioNet sleep-accel): four-stage kappa 0.36 and REM F1 0.575.
These are rough estimates. Pulse's port has not been compared with a sleep lab or with WHOOP's own stages.

**Restorative sleep** = deep + REM, as minutes and as a share of the time asleep. Its level uses NOOP's restorative sub-score (the Rest
composite in `AnalyticsEngine.swift`): `credit = min(1, share ÷ 0.50) × deepFactor`, with `deepFactor = 0.5 + 0.5 × min(1, deep share ÷ 0.13)`,
so a night with almost no deep sleep keeps at most half the credit. NOOP notes healthy adults sit at about 40 to 50%. *Our choice:* the
three steps over the credit: optimal from 0.8 (40% with enough deep), sufficient from 0.6, poor below. It is shown next to the sleep
performance and is not part of its score, so Sleep % doesn't change. The owner's night of 2026-11-17/18: 3h 35m, 39%, sufficient.

## 13. Wear sensor

The band writes readable status lines (CONSOLE_LOGS). Pulse reads three of them: `TIGHTNESS V1: ... loose count = N, loose flag = 0 or 1`,
`IQS7223B: Error reported, error code 0x..` and `CAPSENSE: Error reported while processing command`. WHOOP doesn't document them, so what
they mean is read from their wording. The band splits this text into 50-character packets, so Pulse joins them in order and drops a line
if a packet is missing.

The owner's band also logged `Capsense triggered an off wrist event, but Sigproc controls wear detection`: the capacitive sensor doesn't
decide on its own whether the band is worn.

- A reading counts for 30 minutes. Settings > Band shows No errors or Error, from the two error lines.
- Export all data includes the readings of the last 30 days (`wear_sensor`, format 2), so they can be compared with a night or a workout.
- **The loose flag is not shown or used.** On 2026-11-18 the band reported `loose flag = 1` while the owner's strap was tight, and WHOOP
  doesn't document what the flag measures. Pulse doesn't tell you the strap is loose, and doesn't mark a workout or night as less certain
  because of this flag.

## 14. Daytime stress

NOOP's default day-relative score (`DaytimeStress.swift`, `StressView.swift`). Each waking hour (6 AM to 10 PM) is compared with the
calmest hours of the same day.

1. **An hour counts** with at least 300 seconds of heart rate. Its values are the mean heart rate and the RMSSD of its clean beats
   (at least 20 pairs).
2. **Moving hours are left out.** An hour is left out when at least 30% of its minutes are moving. So is the hour right after one, while
   its heart rate is still more than 8 bpm above the calm reference. Moving hours don't count toward the reference.
3. **Calm reference:** the lower quartile of the day's hourly heart rates and the upper quartile of its hourly RMSSD. With fewer than 4
   hours, the plain mean. The spread is the population standard deviation across the same hours.
4. **Score:** `raw = (HR − calm HR) / spread HR + (calm RMSSD − RMSSD) / spread RMSSD`, then `score = 3 / (1 + e^(−raw))`.
5. **Levels:** Calm below 1, Moderate from 1 to below 2, High from 2. An hour that matches the calm reference scores 1.5, so Moderate is
   the normal middle of the scale.

*Our choices for this band:* a minute is moving at a movement of 30 or more, the same floor as the walk suggestion, because the WHOOP 4.0
data has no NOOP-style gravity reading. Minutes inside a workout also count as moving, and sleep is left out using Pulse's own sleep
times. Time per level is the time measured in each hour, not a full hour. "Right now" is the newest hour scored in the last two clock hours.
Levels stay hidden until 4 hours are scored. NOOP shows them from the first hour, but with two hours one always reads calm and the other
high, whatever the heart rates are.

**Limits:** the score is relative to the day itself, so even a relaxed day has its higher hours, and scores can shift as the day goes on.
Caffeine, heat, a big meal or illness look the same as stress. NOOP's stress score is not a clinical measure, and this one has not been
compared with WHOOP's stress monitor.

## 15. Steps

**What the band gives.** A WHOOP 4.0 sends no step count. Its history (record version 24, about one record a second) holds one 3-axis
motion reading per second in g (two copies of the same vector), next to heart rate and beat intervals; NOOP's decoder of the same record
agrees. A step takes about half a second, so one reading a second can't see each footfall. On 2026-11-19 none of 23,160 readings passed
1.71 g, so the stored reading is smoothed. Pulse keeps, per second, `activity` (§4: the larger of |size − 1 g| and the turn of the
reading's direction, × 500, up to 255) and the wrist's turn in tenths of a degree (`WristAngle`). NOOP's counter needs the step counter
that only the WHOOP 5.0 and MG keep, and its WHOOP 4.0 estimate is all of the day's motion times a factor fitted to a phone's step
count, which counts typing and driving too and which NOOP itself says over-reads on active days. So Pulse finds the walking itself:

1. **A second moves** when it is worn, its activity is at least 40 (about 0.08 g, or a 4.6° turn), and the wrist turned at least 3°
   that second, or the activity is 150 or more.
2. **A second is walking** when, of the worn seconds within 5 seconds either side (at least 8), at least 60% move and at least half
   reach 80. The owner's walking minutes on 2026-11-18 had a median of 117 to 234; sitting stayed under 15 with a few spikes.
3. **A walk** is a run of walking seconds with pauses of up to 3 seconds, lasting at least 12 seconds, or 20 inside a sleep.
4. **Running:** a walking second where at least half the window is at 150 or more and the window's mean heart rate is at least
   `resting + 0.55 × (max − resting)` (resting 60 when unknown). Movement alone can't tell: walking around at home reached 150 in 63% of
   its seconds, at 86 bpm. The first minute of a run, before the heart rate climbs, counts as walking.
5. **Cadence:** 1.8 steps a second walking (108 a minute) and 2.7 running (162), before the personal factor.
6. **NOOP's sleep-aware counter** (`NoopSteps`, ported line for line): the steps become a cumulative counter, one reading per worn second
   with the second's class (still, walk, run). Outside a sleep, and in its awake minutes (§4 step 10), every step counts. Asleep, only a
   coherent walk counts: at least 5 readings over at least 4 seconds with at least 6 steps, no gap over 3 seconds.
7. **A day** is counted hour by hour (each hour starts from the reading before it), and its total is the sum of the hours. With under 90%
   of the day so far measured, the day says how long the band measured, as calories do.

**The step test's 100 Hz count.** The band has a faster stream: command 0x3F (SEND_R10_R11_REALTIME) makes it send, about
once a second, 100 accelerometer samples per axis (packet type 0x2B, re-toolkit/docs/PROTOCOL.md §8b). It exists only while
the app asks for it and, left on, floods Bluetooth and the band's memory, so Pulse turns it off on every connect and on only
for a step test (15 minutes at most). Each unbroken run of seconds is counted with OpenStrap's pedometer, ported line for line
(`OpenStrapPedometer`, MIT): the size of each reading, an 8-sample moving average, peaks in a 33-sample window against a
threshold that follows recent peaks with a 0.10 g dead zone, steps 0.2 to 2 s apart, and 8 regular steps before anything
counts. OpenStrap's own figures: exact on clean synthetic walking at 60 to 180 steps a minute; on OxWalk (39 adults, free
living, camera ground truth) 33% average error at 100 Hz on the wrist, so it is a counter for walks it supervises, not an
all-day source. OpenStrap also measured an earlier "movement minutes × cadence" estimate on one-second data over-reading about
6× on a real day; Pulse's own all-day estimate above is of that kind, with a stricter test for walking, and the Apple Watch
days are what show whether it holds. The step test shows the 100 Hz count next to the owner's and keeps both.

**The personal factor.** The owner's references: a step test (walk, count the steps by hand, Pulse compares the same minutes) and Apple
Watch day totals, typed in. A reference teaches Pulse when the band measured at least 90% of its time and both sides counted at least
100 steps. From 3 references, NOOP's fit (`StepsEstimateEngine.calibrate`): the median of reference ÷ Pulse, each reference weighted by
Pulse's count, with NOOP's confidence. With 1 or 2, their plain median. With none, 1. The factor stays between 0.5 and 2, so one wrong
entry can't make the count absurd. *Our choice:* NOOP withholds steps until 3 days are fitted; Pulse shows its count from the start,
because it is already in steps, and labels it an estimate.

**Checking.** Each reference is compared with what Pulse would have said without it: its factor learned from the other references
only. The error is (Pulse − reference) ÷ reference, and the average leaves out partial days. Targets set by the owner: within 5% on a
500-step test and within 10% of the Apple Watch per day.

**Status:** tested only on simulated wrists (`WristSimulator`: the arm swings once every two steps, each step jolts the wrist, stride
timing wanders by 3%, one reading a second) and on the owner's backup of 2026-11-17/18. Simulated, after one step test: within 4.7% on a
500-step walk at 1.6 to 2.1 steps a second; 0 steps for an hour of typing or of sleep; up to 64 an hour on a rough-road drive and up to
43 an hour talking with the hands. On the backup: 0 steps in the night, at most 30 at the computer (21:30 to 00:30), and a walk found
at 11:27 to 11:39. Not yet compared with a real step test or the Apple Watch. Minutes saved before the wrist's turn was stored use the
older activity value, which reads lower.

## 16. VO₂ max

NOOP's estimate (`FitnessAgeEngine.swift`, `docs/FITNESS_AGE.md`, and the Uth fallback in `WorkoutDetector.swift`), without an exercise test:

1. **The week:** the 7 days ending today. The resting heart rate is the median of the week's nightly values (§3), and a number needs at
   least 4 of the 7 nights.
2. **With age, sex and waist entered**, Nes et al. 2011 (HUNT, waist variant):
   men `100.27 − 0.296 age + 0.226 PA − 0.369 waist − 0.155 RHR`, women `74.74 − 0.247 age + 0.198 PA − 0.259 waist − 0.114 RHR`, ± its
   standard error of 5.70 (men) or 5.14 (women) ml/kg/min. Unspecified sex uses the men's model, as NOOP does.
3. **PA** is NOOP's HUNT activity index, rebuilt from the week (0 to 15 = frequency × intensity × duration): days with a workout
   (0, 1, 2, 3 to 4, 5+ → 0, 0.5, 1, 2.5, 5), the share of the workouts' zone time in zones 4 and 5 (under 15%, under 50%, more → 1, 2, 3),
   and the average workout minutes per active day (under 15, 30, 60, more → 0.10, 0.38, 0.75, 1). *Our choice:* NOOP has this zone path
   and a strain path; Pulse uses the zone path, because NOOP's strain path expects its own 0 to 100 strain, not Pulse's 0 to 21.
4. **Otherwise**, Uth et al. 2004: `15.3 × max heart rate ÷ resting heart rate`, with the profile's max heart rate.

It is a non-exercise estimate with a large error, so the app shows it as an estimate and a trend over weeks. It has not been compared
with a lab test or with WHOOP.
